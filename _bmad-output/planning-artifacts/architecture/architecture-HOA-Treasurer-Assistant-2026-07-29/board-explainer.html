A note for the board · Treasury Watchdog pilot

It cannot move
your money.
Not "will not." Cannot.

This is the one thing worth understanding about the Treasury Watchdog before it starts reviewing the association's records — and it is a claim about how the system is built, not a promise about how it will behave.

The distinction that matters01

Most software that touches money is restricted by permissions. Someone decides the program may look but not touch, and writes that decision down in a settings page. Permissions are a good thing. They are also a setting, and settings can be changed — by a mistake, by a well-meaning administrator, or by someone who should not have had the chance.

The Watchdog is restricted differently. It holds no banking credentials at all. There is no account it can sign into, no payment rail it can reach, no stored key that corresponds to the association's money. The restriction is not a rule the system follows. It is a capability the system was never given.

You cannot misuse a key you do not have. The safety claim here is an absence, and an absence cannot be widened by accident.

Adding that capability later would not be a configuration change or a checkbox. It would require deliberately building a connection that does not currently exist, in code, reviewed and released — the kind of change that leaves a trail and cannot happen quietly.

What it does, and what it doesn't03

It is worth being precise about the boundary, because "read-only" is often used loosely and overstates the case in one direction while understating it in another.

It does not
Read the invoices, statements, and rolls you upload

One clarification, because it is often gotten wrong

The Watchdog does write — to its own database. It has to. It stores the documents you upload, the figures it reads out of them, the alerts it raises, and a permanent log of every question the board asks. What it never does is write outward: into a bank, a payment processor, or the association's accounting platform. Those remain yours alone.

Why the answers can be trusted04

A tool that confidently states a wrong number is worse than no tool, particularly in a meeting. So the Watchdog is built so that it structurally cannot produce a figure it made up.

It does not calculate. Every number in an answer comes from a reviewed query run against the association's records, and before an answer is shown to you it is checked: any figure that does not appear in those results is rejected and the answer is regenerated. If the system cannot support a number with a record, you do not see the number.

Alongside each answer you can open the exact query that produced it, and the rows it returned. When a resident disputes a balance in a meeting, that is what you read from — the record, not the software's opinion of the record.

Questions you may be asked05

"So an AI has access to our bank account?"

No. It has never been given banking credentials, and there is no setting that would grant them. It sees the documents the board chooses to upload and nothing else.

"What if it makes a mistake and pays the wrong vendor?"

It cannot pay anyone. Payment still happens the way it happens today — a board member, signed into the bank, authorizing it. The Watchdog's role ends at telling you something looks wrong before you do that.

"Who can see our financial records?"

The board members you authorize. Every query run against the records is logged permanently with who asked and what was returned, so access itself is auditable.

"Can it change my balance?"

No. It reads the assessment roll and payment records the board uploads and reports what they show. Correcting a balance is a board action taken in the association's own books.

"Is our data sitting on someone else's computers?"

Yes — the pilot runs on managed cloud infrastructure rather than a server in the clubhouse. That is a real and deliberate trade-off, and the board should weigh it knowingly rather than discover it later.

Scope of this note

This describes how the pilot system is built. It is not legal advice and makes no claim about whether any particular practice satisfies a board's obligations under Florida law or the association's governing documents. Directors should take those questions to the association's counsel.
